Rack placement and collective performance#

Explanation — background and reasoning. No instructions.

On a rack-scale NVLink system with a leaf-spine scale-out fabric, the first performance setting of a multi-rack job is which racks it runs on. Tune NCCL and the NIC first and you are adjusting the last 1-2% of a result whose first 10% was decided by placement. This page says why, and what it means for every cluster Rack Status runs on. The procedure is in how to place a multi-rack job; the measured settings per job size are in collective settings by job size.

Three layers, three domains#

A job’s traffic crosses up to three domains, and each has a different bandwidth and a different owner:

Domain

What it is

Who carries the traffic

Rack

One NVLink domain (an NVL72 rack, 18 compute trays, 72 GPUs)

NVLink switches, inside the rack

Scalable unit (SU)

The racks one set of leaf switches serves

Leaf switches: rack to rack inside the SU

Fabric

Every SU

Spine (and super-spine) switches: SU to SU

The Rack Status record already models the middle layer: a cluster’s scalable_units is the SU-to-rack map. The outer layer is not in the record; it lives in the switches.

Why NVLink matters less as the job grows#

All-to-all sends an equal share of data from every GPU to every other GPU. With R racks in the job, only 1/R of a GPU’s traffic stays on NVLink; the rest crosses the scale-out fabric. At 2 racks half of it is local, at 8 racks one eighth, at 15 racks under 7%. Small jobs are NVLink-bound and forgiving of placement; large jobs are fabric-bound and are not.

So “keep the rack whole” never stops mattering, but what it protects changes. Spreading the same number of nodes over many partial racks multiplies the fabric share and fragments the NVLink domains. On Nscale NC, taking the best 16 nodes per SU from any rack (95 rack fragments) for 1024 GPUs read 79.0 GB/s against 92.1 on whole racks, and with MNNVL on the job hung at initialisation.

Why two racks in one SU cost more than two racks in two SUs#

Two racks in the same SU exchange their traffic through the same leaf switches, so their flows share leaf ports and leaf buffers. Two racks in different SUs exchange theirs through the spine, which on a well-built fabric has more aggregate capacity between leaf groups than one leaf group has inside itself for this pattern. On Nscale NC at 128 GPUs, two racks in one SU read 130-133 GB/s all-to-all and two racks in two SUs read 175-180, with the switches pausing the NICs about 200 times more often in the same-SU case. No NCCL or NIC setting closed that gap; the switches were the limit.

The rule that follows is simple and held at every size tested: one rack per SU. The passing 128-GPU runs used 2 racks in 2 SUs, the 256-GPU runs 4 in 4, the 512-GPU run 8 in 8, and the 1024-GPU run 15 in 15.

Why the rack within each SU still matters#

With one rack per SU and identical settings, three different rack sets at 1024 GPUs read 91.1, 93.0 and 94.6 GB/s; one set run twice read 94.51 and 94.61. Adding racks one at a time to a passing 512-GPU job was not monotonic (102, 71, 82, 95, 83, 92.5 GB/s), and single rounds collapsed on identical nodes. A node or rack fault would be repeatable; this is not. It is the signature of how ECMP hashing and adaptive routing place flows on the spine for a given set of endpoints. That is likely, not proven: Rack Status sees the nodes and the leaves, not the spine.

The practical consequence is that a large job’s result is a property of its rack set, so the rack set is part of the configuration and is recorded with the result. Measure a few one-rack-per-SU sets and keep the best one; the cluster owner should look at the spine so that any set would do.

Why this is not a congestion-control problem#

NIC congestion control (on Spectrum-X, the RTT-based ztr_rttcc algorithm) does matter, but as a setting that has to be right rather than as the limiter. On Nscale NC:

  • At 128 and 256 GPUs, CC off measured best and the handover runs used it.

  • At 512 and above, CC must be on: off halves 512-GPU all-to-all. The MoE profile beats the site profile, which is the deployment guide’s recommendation, by about 4% at 1024.

  • Once CC is on with the MoE profile, making it less aggressive in four different ways (later reaction, a softer first cut, faster recovery, a bigger increase) moved 1024 by under 0.5%. If CC were throttling the job, one of those would have helped.

  • At 1024 the switches paused the NICs about 400 times less often per second than at 512, with zero ECN marks, drops and retransmissions, for a lower result. The job is not losing time to pauses.

The same is true of NCCL settings: once the buffer is right (8 MB, not 16 MB), queue pairs, P2P chunk size, channel count and adaptive routing together spanned under 2% at 1024. Placement spanned 8%.

Why the hostname is not the source of truth#

Rack letters and hostnames describe where a rack stands, not which leaves it is cabled to. On Nscale NC, SU35’s racks U26 and X26 stand in the rows of SU20 and SU32, and SU36’s T1 in the row of SU33 and SU34, yet every one of their ports lands on their own SU’s leaves. That happened to match the -suNN hostname suffix and the cluster record, but only reading the switch neighbours proved it. The leaves are the ground truth; LLDP on the node reads them (see the how-to).

What this means for any cluster#

  • Treat placement as the first tuning parameter and record it with every multi-rack result.

  • Keep the scalable_units map on the cluster record correct, and verify it against LLDP once per cluster. Every placement decision and every handover report is built on it.

  • Tune NCCL and congestion control per job size, on a placement that is already right. A setting measured on a bad rack set measures the rack set.

  • A gap that placement and settings cannot close is a switch question (spine capacity, hashing, adaptive routing, link state). Hand it to the network owner with the rack sets and job ids that show it.
